Why renewing your MCA is usually a bad idea

Halfway through paying off an advance, your funder calls with good news: you qualify for more. It sounds like a reward for paying on time. In reality, a renewal is one of the most expensive moves you can make, because you pay fees all over again on money you already owed.

How a renewal works

The funder offers you a new, larger advance. Part of it is used to pay off what you still owe on the current one, and you get whatever is left. The problem is that the new factor rate applies to the whole new amount, including the part that just pays off your old balance. And that old balance already includes the first funder's fees.

A real-world example

  • Your first advance: you receive $50,000 at a 1.45 factor rate. You agree to pay back $72,500.
  • A few months in: you've paid back $36,250. You still owe $36,250.
  • The renewal offer: $60,000 at the same 1.45 factor rate, so you'll pay back $87,000. There's a $3,000 fee taken off the top.
  • Where the money goes: $36,250 goes straight back to the funder to clear your old balance, and $3,000 goes to fees. Only $20,750 actually reaches your account.

So you've agreed to pay $87,000 to get $20,750 of new cash. Part of what you're paying for is the $36,250 you already owed, and about $11,250 of that was the first advance's fee. You're now being charged a new fee on top of the old fee: interest on interest.

Renewing vs. finishing the first advance

  • Without the renewal: you pay the remaining $36,250 and you're done. Total paid: $72,500 for $50,000 received.
  • With the renewal: you've paid $36,250 plus $87,000, a total of $123,250 for $70,750 received.
  • The renewal cost you an extra $50,750 in payments to get $20,750 more cash.

And it rarely stops at one. Many owners renew again a few months later, and each round adds another layer of fees to the balance.

What that looks like as an APR

Funders don't call any of this interest. They call it a factor rate, a purchase price or a fee, and that's exactly how the real cost stays hidden. Put the same numbers into APR terms, the way a bank loan has to show its cost, and the picture changes:

  • The first advance paid back over 12 months: a 1.45 factor rate works out to roughly 79% APR.
  • The same advance paid back over 6 months: about 158% APR. The fee is the same $22,500, but you pay it in half the time, so the APR doubles.
  • The renewal, paid back over 6 months: the extra cost of getting $20,750 in new cash works out to roughly 280% APR.

The shorter the term, the higher the true rate. And because payments come out every business day, you're paying down the balance from day one while being charged as if you had the full amount the whole time.

APRs are estimates based on equal daily payments over 21 business days a month. Your contract's actual terms may differ.

Why funders push renewals

  • A business that pays on time is the easiest customer to sell more money to.
  • The renewal pays off the old balance in full, so the funder collects the entire first fee right away, with no discount for early payoff in most contracts.
  • Brokers are often paid a new commission on every renewal (see how brokers are paid).
  • Each renewal makes the balance bigger and the payments last longer, which keeps you tied to the funder.
