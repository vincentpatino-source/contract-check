UCC liens from MCA funders: what they are and how to get them removed

Almost every MCA funder files a UCC lien against your business. Most owners don't notice until a bank turns them down for a loan, or a customer calls to say a funder told them to send payments somewhere else.

What a UCC lien is

A UCC-1 financing statement is a public notice filed with your state, usually the Secretary of State. It tells the world that the funder claims rights in your business's receivables, and often in other business assets too. Anyone can look it up, including lenders checking your credit.

How it affects your business

  • It can block new financing. Banks and SBA lenders see the lien and often won't lend until it's gone.
  • It's the basis for lien notices. If there's a dispute, a funder may send notices to your card processor or customers telling them to pay the funder directly. Those notices can cut off your cash flow overnight.
  • It can outlast the advance. A UCC filing generally stays on record for five years unless it's ended sooner, even after the advance is paid or settled.

Getting it removed

When an advance is paid off or settled, the funder should file a UCC-3 termination. Under the Uniform Commercial Code, once the debt is satisfied and you send a written demand, the funder generally has 20 days to file it. If it doesn't, the law gives you ways to have it terminated.

In a settlement, the lien release should be written into the agreement itself, so it's not left to the funder's goodwill. Getting it in writing is one of the details your attorney handles.

If your customers got a lien notice

Don't ignore it and don't argue with the funder yourself. Call your customer, tell them you're handling it, and get help right away. An attorney licensed in your state can respond to the funder and, depending on the facts, challenge notices that go beyond what the contract or the law allows.
