What happens if you stop paying your MCA?

When the daily or weekly debits get to be too much, stopping payments can feel like the only option. What happens next depends on your contract, your state and, most of all, whether you have a plan and the right people in place before you stop.

Why stopping without a plan is risky

Most MCA contracts are written so that a missed or blocked payment can be treated as a default. When that happens, funders often move quickly:

  • The contract may make the full remaining balance due at once, and many add default fees and collection costs on top.
  • The funder may file a lawsuit, often in New York, or try to use a confession of judgment where the law allows it.
  • Many funders send notices to your card processor or customers under their UCC filing, telling them to pay the funder directly.
  • After a judgment, a funder may try to freeze your bank account.

None of this is automatic, and much of it can be challenged or negotiated. But it moves fast, which is why the order matters: get help first, then change your payments.

What can count as a missed payment

It isn't only about running out of money. Under many contracts, these can also be treated as a missed payment:

  • Placing a stop payment or blocking the funder's debits at your bank
  • Moving your deposits to a new bank account
  • Switching card processors without telling the funder
  • A debit that bounces or only partly clears

If you're thinking about any of these, talk to an MCA debt restructuring expert first.

Stopping isn't always a breach

A merchant cash advance is supposed to be a purchase of your future sales, not a loan. If your sales really dropped, paying less may be exactly what the contract allows. Many agreements include a reconciliation right to adjust payments to your actual revenue, and courts look closely at whether the funder truly shares the risk of a slow period. When it doesn't, the deal may be treated as a loan in disguise, which can change everything.

A safer way to lower your payments

Instead of simply stopping, the better path is to restructure with professionals on your side:

  • Your contracts and bank statements are reviewed first, so you know where you stand with each funder.
  • You're assigned an attorney licensed in your state who can respond if a funder sues.
  • Restructuring specialists work with all of your funders at once, so one doesn't jump ahead of the others.
  • Payments are typically 50 to 80% lower than what you're paying now, with complete legal protection included.

Having an attorney on your side doesn't make this the expensive option. It usually makes it the cheaper one. On average, our clients pay less in total than what their funders say they owe, and that already includes attorney fees and every other cost of settling the debt. Compare that with paying every advance in full, plus any default fees if you fall behind.

Don't stop paying on your own

Funders have collection attorneys ready to act the day a payment is missed. Having professionals involved before you change your payments gives you protection and leverage, and funders often negotiate differently once they see it.
