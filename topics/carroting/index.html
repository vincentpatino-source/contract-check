Carroting: the broker promise of a better loan later

It's one of the most common ways business owners end up in an MCA. A broker dangles a carrot: take this cash advance now, make a few months of payments, and you'll qualify for a low-rate line of credit or an SBA loan. Then the better loan never shows up.

How the pitch usually goes

  • The carrot: "We can get you an SBA loan or a low-interest line of credit. You just need to build some payment history first."
  • The stick: the only thing actually funded is an expensive MCA with daily or weekly payments.
  • The repeat: when the better loan doesn't come through, the next offer is often another advance or a renewal, and the broker earns another commission.

A federal case: charged with carroting

In April 2025, the U.S. Attorney's Office for New Jersey announced charges against eight people over an alleged scheme that targeted small business owners nationwide. The industry calls this kind of scheme carroting. Prosecutors say that starting in June 2020 the group contacted owners by email and phone and charged them money in return for the promise of a large line of credit that never came, taking millions of dollars from thousands of victims. The charges are accusations, and the defendants are presumed innocent unless proven guilty.

Carroting comes in two forms: taking upfront fees for a loan that never arrives, and pushing an expensive MCA now with the promise of cheap financing later. Both rely on the same bait.

Why the better loan rarely materializes

SBA loans and bank lines of credit look closely at cash flow and existing debt. Daily MCA payments eat into cash flow and add debt, which can make it harder, not easier, to qualify. An MCA on your bank statements can work against the very loan you were promised.

Protect yourself

  • Get any promise of future financing in writing, from the actual lender, with the terms.
  • Ask who the lender is and whether you've actually been approved, not just "pre-qualified".
  • Read the MCA contract itself. A broker's promises usually aren't part of it, and most contracts say you didn't rely on anything outside the agreement.
  • Keep texts and emails. If you were misled, they can matter later.

Don't face funders alone

Many MCA funders are backed by investor capital, including hedge funds and private lenders, and they have experienced collection attorneys on call. Outside a handful of states, the industry is lightly regulated, and it can feel like the Wild West. Regulators have even banned some funders for deceiving and threatening business owners. You need someone on your side who knows how these companies operate.

  • Experienced professionals know which funders negotiate and which tactics are bluffs.
  • An attorney licensed in your state can respond to lawsuits, judgments and frozen accounts.
  • Funders often negotiate differently once a professional is involved.

Do the rules address this?

Misleading financing pitches can violate federal and state laws against deceptive practices. The FTC has brought cases against MCA companies for misleading small businesses. Some states also regulate brokers directly: Kansas, for example, prohibits brokers from making false or misleading statements, and several states require MCA brokers to register. See Brokers (ISOs).
