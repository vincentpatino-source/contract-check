Reverse consolidations explained

A reverse consolidation is pitched as relief from several MCAs. Instead of paying off your other funders, the new funder sends you weekly deposits so you can keep up with them, while you make payments back to the new funder.

Example

Picture a restaurant owner, call her Maria, paying three funders every business day. A broker calls with a fix: a new funder will send her money every week to keep up with those payments. It feels like a lifeline. What the broker doesn't stress is that the weekly deposits get smaller, Maria now owes a fourth funder, and the other three are still there.

It's a bit like borrowing from a fourth friend to pay back three others, except the new friend hands you less every week and expects back a lot more than he lent.

How the money moves

  • The new funder pays you in installments, usually weekly, on a disbursement schedule in the contract.
  • Those deposits often get smaller over time.
  • At the same time, you make daily or weekly payments to the new funder for a total that's much higher than what it sends you.

Regular consolidation

  • Pays off your existing advances
  • You're left with one payment
  • Old funders are done

Reverse consolidation

  • Sends you weekly cash instead
  • Your old advances stay open
  • You add one more funder to pay

What it really costs: a simple example

  • Before: you owe $150,000 to three funders, and the payments are more than you can keep up with.
  • The deal: a new funder agrees to send you $150,000 in weekly deposits to cover those payments. In return, you agree to pay it back $225,000.
  • Where the money goes: every deposit goes straight out to your three original funders. You never get to spend a dollar of it on your business.
  • After: your old funders get paid, and you now owe $225,000 to the new one.

Your debt just grew by $75,000, for money you never got to use. And that's the best case. If the weekly deposits get smaller or stop early, which is common, you're back to paying the original funders yourself while still owing the new one. That's how so many owners with a reverse consolidation end up $200,000 or more in debt.

The risk

Your existing advances usually stay in place, so you're now paying one more funder on top of the others. When the deposits shrink or stop, many businesses end up deeper in debt than before.

Before signing, compare the total you'll receive with the total you'll pay back, and check whether the deposits can be cut or stopped.
