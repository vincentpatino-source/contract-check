MCA factor rates explained, and what they really cost in APR

A factor rate like 1.3 or 1.45 looks small and simple. That's the point. It hides how expensive a merchant cash advance really is, because it says nothing about how fast you have to pay it back.

What a factor rate is

A factor rate is the number your advance is multiplied by to get what you owe. Get $50,000 at a 1.35 factor rate, and you owe $67,500. The $17,500 difference is the cost of the advance. It's fixed on day one, so paying early usually saves you nothing.

Funders don't call it interest. MCAs are written as a purchase of your future sales, not a loan, so they don't have to show an interest rate or an APR the way a bank loan does in most states.

Why a factor rate is not an interest rate

  • Time is missing. A 1.35 factor rate costs the same $17,500 whether you pay it back over 12 months or 4. Paid in 4 months, it's three times as expensive per year.
  • You start paying the next day. Daily or weekly debits shrink what you actually have long before the term is up, but the cost is charged on the full amount the whole time.
  • Fees come on top. Origination, underwriting and other fees are often taken out before you get the money, so you receive less than the advance amount but still owe the full payback.

Factor rates in APR terms

Here is roughly what common factor rates work out to as an APR, with equal payments every business day and no extra fees. Fees push the real number even higher.

  • 1.20: about 38% APR over 12 months, 75% over 6 months, 112% over 4 months
  • 1.30: about 55% APR over 12 months, 109% over 6 months, 163% over 4 months
  • 1.40: about 71% APR over 12 months, 142% over 6 months, 213% over 4 months
  • 1.50: about 87% APR over 12 months, 174% over 6 months, 260% over 4 months

For comparison, a typical bank or SBA business loan is in the single or low double digits. Even the cheapest line in this list costs several times more.

States that make funders show the cost

California and New York now require many commercial financing providers, including MCA funders, to give you a disclosure with an estimated APR before you sign, and several other states have passed similar disclosure laws. If you didn't get one, or the numbers on it don't match your contract, that's worth having reviewed. See your state's MCA page for what applies to you.
