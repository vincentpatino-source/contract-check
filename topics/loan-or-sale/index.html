Is your merchant cash advance really a loan in disguise?

Every MCA contract says it isn't a loan. It's written as a purchase of your future sales. But courts look at how the deal actually works, not just what it's called, and a growing number have found that MCAs were loans in disguise.

Why the label matters

If an MCA is a true sale, the funder takes the risk that your sales slow down or stop. If it's really a loan, it can be subject to usury laws that cap interest rates, and an agreement charging far above those caps may not be enforceable.

The three questions courts usually ask

Under New York law, which governs many MCA contracts, courts generally look at three things:

  • Reconciliation: Does the contract let your payment go down when your sales drop, and does that actually work in practice?
  • Term: Does the deal have a set end date, or does it really last until the funder collects, however long that takes?
  • Recourse: Can the funder still come after you if the business fails or files for bankruptcy?

No single answer decides the case. Courts look at the deal as a whole, including whether the funder carried any real risk of not being paid.

What courts have found recently

In 2026, a New York bankruptcy court recharacterized 19 merchant cash advance agreements as usurious loans despite contract language saying they weren't loans. A New York appeals court also held in 2026 that MCA agreements in the Attorney General's case against Richmond Capital Group were properly treated as loans subject to usury limits.

Other courts have upheld MCAs as true sales when the reconciliation right was real and the funder truly bore the risk. The outcome depends on your contract's wording and how the funder actually behaved.
