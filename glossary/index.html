MCA legal terms glossary

The legal and industry terms you'll run into with a merchant cash advance, explained in plain English.

Speak with a restructuring expert Check my contract free Debt calculator
Attorney General
A state's top law enforcement officer, who can sue MCA funders for illegal practices. Learn more →
Automatic stay
The pause on most collection activity that starts when a bankruptcy is filed. Learn more →
Bankruptcy restrictions
Contract terms that try to stop you from filing bankruptcy or punish you if you do. Courts generally don't enforce advance promises not to file. Learn more →
BCL § 1312(a)
A New York law that blocks an out-of-state company doing business in New York without authority from maintaining a lawsuit there until it's authorized. Learn more →
BCL § 1314
A New York law limiting when one non-New York company can sue another non-New York company in New York courts. Learn more →
Carroting
Luring business owners with the promise of a low-rate loan or line of credit that never materializes, either to collect upfront fees or to sell an expensive MCA. Learn more →
CFPB
The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, a federal agency. Its 2026 small business lending data rule excludes merchant cash advances. Learn more →
Commercial Division
A part of the New York State Supreme Court that handles business disputes, where many MCA cases are heard. Learn more →
Commercial financing disclosure law
A state law requiring funders to show you the cost and terms of an MCA before you sign. In effect in states including California, New York, Texas and Florida. Learn more →
Confession of judgment (COJ)
A document signed in advance that lets a funder get a court judgment against you without a lawsuit. Banned or limited in several states, including Florida, Texas and Virginia. Learn more →
CPLR 317
A New York rule that can let you defend a case after a default if you were not personally served and didn't actually receive notice in time. Learn more →
Default fee
A charge added to your balance when the funder says you broke the contract, such as missing or reducing payments. Learn more →
Default judgment
A judgment entered because the defendant didn't respond to the lawsuit in time. Learn more →
Factor rate
The multiplier used to set what you pay back. A 1.4 factor rate on $50,000 means paying back $70,000. It is not an interest rate and usually hides a much higher yearly cost. Learn more →
Injunction
A court order requiring someone to do, or stop doing, something. Learn more →
ISO (broker)
An independent sales organization: a broker who sells MCAs and is usually paid a commission built into your cost. Learn more →
Jurisdiction
A court's power to decide a case involving you or your business. Learn more →
Merchant cash advance (MCA)
Money given to a business up front in exchange for a larger amount of its future sales, usually collected through daily or weekly bank debits.
Mutual mistake
A defense that both sides signed based on the same wrong understanding of a key fact. Learn more →
Purchased amount (total payback)
The total the funder is entitled to collect from you, including its profit. Learn more →
Receivership
When a court appoints a neutral person to take control of a company's assets, sometimes an MCA funder shut down by regulators. Learn more →
Recharacterization
When a court decides an MCA labeled as a sale is really a loan. Learn more →
Reconciliation
A contract process for adjusting your payment to match your actual sales. Funders often add a default fee once a reduced payment is made. Learn more →
Restraining notice (CPLR 5222)
A New York notice, issued after a judgment, that freezes up to twice the judgment amount in your bank account. Learn more →
Return date
The hearing date by which you must respond to a motion, such as a motion for summary judgment. Learn more →
Reverse consolidation
An advance that pays you in weekly installments, often to keep up with other advances, while you make payments back to the new funder. Learn more →
RICO
A federal law that, among other things, covers collecting unlawful debts such as criminally usurious loans. Learn more →
Service of process (CPLR 308)
How lawsuit papers are delivered to you under New York law, such as in person, or left with someone and mailed. Learn more →
Specified percentage (holdback)
The share of your sales the funder is supposed to collect. Many contracts use a fixed daily payment instead. Learn more →
Stacking
Taking more than one MCA at the same time. Many contracts ban it, but funders often fund businesses that already have other advances.
Summary judgment
A ruling that decides a case without a trial when the court finds no real dispute over the key facts. Learn more →
Summons and complaint
The papers that start a lawsuit. They come with a deadline to respond. Learn more →
True sale
A deal where the funder really buys your future sales and takes the risk they won't come in. If it isn't a true sale, the MCA may be a loan in disguise. Learn more →
UCC lien (UCC-1 filing)
A public filing that gives the funder a claim on your business's receivables or other assets. Funders sometimes use it to send notices to your customers. Learn more →
Unconscionability
A legal defense that a contract or term is so one-sided and unfairly obtained that a court won't enforce it. Learn more →
Usury
Charging interest above the legal limit. In New York, civil usury is above 16% a year and criminal usury is above 25%. Learn more →
Vacating a judgment
Asking a court to cancel a judgment, for example because you were never properly served. Learn more →
Venue and transfer (CPLR 510)
Venue is the county where a case is heard. New York law allows a case to be moved to a different county in certain situations. Learn more →
