Does your contract say "New York law"? Read this even if you're not in New York

Look for a "governing law" or "choice of law" clause in your agreement. Many MCA contracts say New York law applies and that disputes go to New York courts, no matter where the business is located. If yours does, the New York rules on this page may apply to your contract even if you've never been to New York.

New York's rules for merchant cash advances

New York's Commercial Finance Disclosure Law covers merchant cash advances up to $2.5 million, one of the highest limits in the country.

  • Cost disclosures with an estimated APR. Since August 1, 2023, funders must give you a written disclosure before you sign, including an estimated APR, the total you'll repay, your payment amounts and any prepayment terms.
  • Covers deals up to $2.5 million. Funders that make five or fewer deals in New York in a year are exempt.
  • Renewals must be disclosed. If part of a new advance goes to paying off unpaid charges on an earlier one, the funder has to tell you. This is often called "double dipping."
  • Who oversees it: the New York State Department of Financial Services (DFS).
  • Confessions of judgment. Since August 30, 2019 (CPLR 3218), a confession of judgment can only be filed in the New York county where the business owner lived when it was signed, or lives when it's filed. That largely ended their use against businesses outside New York. For New York residents they can still be used, but they must be filed within three years and follow the statute strictly. If you signed one, our free review checks whether it meets those requirements, and your assigned attorney can act on it.

What New York regulators and lawmakers have done about MCAs

  • New York Attorney General vs. Yellowstone Capital (2025). The Attorney General reached a $1.065 billion settlement with Yellowstone Capital and 24 related companies over what it called illegal high-interest loans disguised as merchant cash advances. More than $534 million in debts owed by over 18,000 small businesses was canceled, and the companies were banned from the MCA business.
  • FAIR Business Practices Act (effective February 17, 2026). New York expanded its main consumer protection law, General Business Law § 349, to ban "unfair" and "abusive" practices, not just deceptive ones, and made clear the Attorney General can act to protect small businesses, not just consumers. Only the Attorney General can bring claims for unfair or abusive practices; businesses can still sue over deceptive ones.
  • Bills to bring MCAs under the usury cap. Bills introduced in the New York legislature would cap MCA costs at 25% or write MCAs directly into the criminal usury law. As of November 2026 these are proposals, not law.
  • FTC vs. Yellowstone Capital. The Federal Trade Commission charged Yellowstone with taking money from businesses' bank accounts without permission. Yellowstone paid more than $9.8 million, and the FTC sent refund checks to 7,731 small businesses.
  • FTC vs. RCG Advances. The FTC alleged RCG Advances deceived and threatened small businesses. A court order permanently banned the company and its owner from the merchant cash advance industry.

When New York courts treat an MCA as a loan

An MCA is written as a purchase of your future sales, not a loan, so on paper the interest-rate limits don't apply. But New York courts look at how the deal actually works, not what it's called. If the funder takes on no real risk that your sales drop, a court can treat the advance as a loan, and then New York's usury laws come into play.

  • The three-factor test. New York courts ask (1) whether the contract has a real reconciliation provision that adjusts payments when sales fall, (2) whether the contract has a fixed end date, and (3) whether the funder can still collect if the business files for bankruptcy. This comes from LG Funding v. United Senior Properties of Olathe (Appellate Division, 2d Dept., 2020). No single factor decides it.
  • The 25% criminal usury cap. New York makes it a crime to knowingly charge more than 25% a year on a loan (Penal Law § 190.40). Businesses normally can't raise usury as a defense, but corporations and LLCs can raise criminal usury (General Obligations Law § 5-521(3)). Most MCAs, figured as an annual rate, run far above 25%. See usury and MCAs.
  • A usurious loan is void. In Adar Bays v. GeneSYS ID (2021), New York's highest court held that a criminally usurious loan is void from the start, meaning neither the principal nor the interest can be collected.
  • It often comes down to the facts. In February 2026, the Appellate Division, First Department, ruled in Kapitus Servicing v. Suburban Waste Services that whether the agreements were loans was a question of fact, so neither side won without a trial. Whether a reconciliation clause was ever honored in practice can matter as much as what the contract says.

Questions from New York business owners

Are merchant cash advances legal in New York?

Yes. Merchant cash advances are legal in New York when they are a true purchase of future sales. But New York courts look past the label. If the funder takes on no real risk, a court can treat the advance as a loan, and loans above 25% a year are criminally usurious in New York.

My business isn't in New York. Why does my contract say New York law?

Many MCA funders are based in New York and write their contracts under New York law, with New York courts named for disputes. It can, if a court decides the advance is really a loan.
